## The Faith Sellers

“So great was the amount of booty that came into the hands of Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn al-Ayyūbī that it could not be counted. The number of prisoners of war was likewise beyond calculation.”

Rather than celebrating his hard-won victories, Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn (Saladin) continued his military campaigns without rest, and captured several additional villages, including one famously known at the time as Gaza (Ghāzzah).

One day, in a region nearby, Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn al-Ayyūbī was resting in the tent of Amīr Jawād al-Asadī.¹ Though weakened by fatigue he had not removed the turban he always wore—both to protect his head from the heat of the sun and from enemy blades on the battlefield. Outside the tent, his guards remained fully alert. The commander of the guards stepped away briefly. One of the guards peered through the tent curtain and saw the great standard-bearer of Islam lying on his back, asleep. The guards exchanged glances. Several distracted their companions, while one advanced silently toward the tent. He drew his dagger from his belt and moved with great swiftness toward Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn, lunging at him like a wildcat.

As the dagger rose, Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn shifted his body. It is unclear whether the blow was aimed at the heart or chest, but the dagger instead struck the upper folds of his turban—missing his head by the width of a single hair. The turban fell.

Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn sprang up instantly. This was not unfamiliar to him—he had already survived two similar attacks. Nor was he surprised that the attacker had disguised themselves as one of his own guards. The attacker had no time even to breathe; in their attempt to strike, they exposed themselves. Ṣalāḥ ad‑Dīn Ayyūbī struck him with a powerful blow. The attacker’s jaw was broken. He fell backward screaming. The dagger remained stuck in the turban.

At the same time, two more guards entered the tent with swords in their hands. Ṣalāḥ ad‑Dīn Ayyūbī ordered them to spare the man’s life, but both of them turned their swords against Ṣalāḥ ad‑Dīn Ayyūbī.

For nearly one or two minutes, Ṣalāḥ ad‑Dīn Ayyūbī fought against both swords with only one dagger, until all the guards entered the tent. They were extremely astonished when they saw Ṣalāḥ ad‑Dīn Ayyūbī. It then became known that among his guards, seven men were enemies. They had been divided into two groups and deceived one another. Some were enemies and some were friends, and on this basis they had carried out the attack.

Some were killed, some fled, and finally one of the guards, after being wounded, fell unconscious on the ground. It then became known that this group belonged to the Sultan and that they were called **fidāʾiyān**. Their duty was to assassinate Ṣalāḥ ad‑Dīn Ayyūbī. For this purpose, they had sent one fidāʾī into the tent first.

When the situation inside the tent changed, the others also entered. The main guards entered the tent as well. Ṣalāḥ ad‑Dīn Ayyūbī was saved from the assassination attempt.

Placing the tip of his sword on the chest of the first attacker, Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn demanded to know who he was and who had sent him. Promising mercy in exchange for the truth, the man confessed that he was a fidāʾī, sent by Kumushtegin (also rendered Gumushtegin), a regional governor under al-Ṣāliḥ, for this purpose.¹².

*As the assassin’s confession echoed in the quiet tent, the weight of the betrayal became clear. The "Faith Sellers" were no longer just a distant threat whispered about in court; they were inside his own camp, wearing the uniforms of his protectors. To understand how the situation became so dire, we must look back at the life of the man they so desperately wanted to kill, but let's have a look at what was going on? — a man whose refusal to be corrupted left his enemies with no choice but the blade.*
